Livestock on carriageway · decision system

GauKavach An acoustic deterrent that is only allowed to act inside the envelope the evidence supports.

The published evidence that ultrasound alone moves cattle is weak — one prototype paper. Most systems built on it quietly overclaim. This one encodes the weakness as hard constraints: it computes what physics permits, refuses what welfare forbids, and hands the incident to a human the moment sound stops being justified.

Q1

“Isn’t this just another ultrasonic buzzer?”

No — because the buzzer is the second thing it does. GauKavach separates the intervention whose mechanism is proven from the one that is not, and never lets the second stand in for the first.

Layer 1 · Traffic response Proven mechanism

Fires the instant livestock is detected, at any distance, regardless of whether sound can reach the animal. Upstream variable message sign, signal hold, advisory speed drop, dispatch ticket. Drivers who are warned about an obstruction slow down — this is not in dispute.

Layer 2 · Acoustic nudge Hypothesis

Only inside the computed envelope, only on an approaching animal, only at the minimum sufficient level, only with no person or non-target species in the cone. Three attempts maximum, then it stops permanently and escalates.

Why the split matters

Umstatter et al. (2013) showed broadcast audio can shift cattle location but explicitly failed to achieve stock-proof containment. A system that keeps emitting until the animal moves is arguing with its own citation. Ours has a defined give-up point.

Q2

“Where did your numbers come from?”

Every constant that can influence an emission is declared in a registry with a source and an evidence grade. A number that is our own guess is graded as our own guess and is structurally forbidden from authorising emission on its own.

ConstantValueGradeSrcWhy this number
The registry caught us once

Our carrier optimiser originally selected 20 kHz. OSHA states plainly that 20 kHz is not a guaranteed silent frequency — so the optimiser was contradicting a source we had cited. The threshold that let it happen was an undeclared magic number. Declaring it forced the value down to 2%, and selection moved to 22 kHz.

Q3

“What about dogs, cats, goats — anything that isn’t a cow?”

This question broke our original design, so it now drives it. Ultrasonic deterrents are sold on the premise that the frequency is species-selective. We checked that premise against comparative audiograms measured at the same 60 dB SPL criterion as the cattle data — and it is not merely unproven. It is backwards.

SpeciesGroupHearing limitOctaves inside range
The finding that reshaped the architecture

Consequence: selectivity cannot come from the frequency. It can only come from the detector. Every non-target veto in the system exists because of this table — and a goat flock beside the road is not an incidental bystander, it is a more sensitive receiver than the target.

Household animals · absolute veto

  • Dogs hear to 45 kHz and may orient at the source rather than away — with documented risk of redirected aggression toward nearby people. Hard stop, never a weighting.
  • Cats hear to 85 kHz, nearly two octaves inside range. The most over-exposed animal at any site.
  • Poultry we checked and cleared: chicken hearing tops out near 7 kHz. Recorded so the register states what is not at risk, not only what is.

Flocks · refuse and escalate

  • Above 3 grouped conspecifics the system refuses outright. Responses are socially facilitated, so a startle propagates through the group rather than staying with one animal.
  • A flock moving as one body in an unpredictable direction is a worse road hazard than the original blockage. A herd is a dispatch problem, not an acoustic one.
  • Known gap: COCO has no goat class. Goats detect as “sheep” or not at all. The ambiguous label maps to both species and the stricter profile wins — declared, not hidden.
Also checked, also uncomfortable

Children. High-frequency hearing declines with age, so a 22 kHz carrier chosen because adults can’t hear it may be plainly audible to a child — commercial anti-loitering devices exploit exactly that gap near 17.4 kHz. Near a school or dense housing the band floor rises to 25 kHz.

Bats. Several insectivorous bats echolocate at 20–35 kHz. This isn’t a coincidence: the atmospheric-absorption paper this whole system depends on is itself a bat echolocation study. Mitigation is sub-second event-triggered bursts only. Residual risk: unquantified, no bat survey done.

Q4

“Could it make an animal behave violently, or push it into traffic?”

Yes — and those are the two hazards we rate catastrophic. An animal flees away from an aversive source. If the emitter sits so that “away” means “across the road”, the device causes the exact collision it exists to prevent. So flight geometry is computed in metric ground coordinates and checked before the acoustics are even consulted.

The flight-vector check

# ray-cast 25 m along the flight vector
flight = site.flight_assessment(pt, 25.0)

This check alone proves something we did not set out to find: GauKavach is an approach deterrent, not a road-clearer. An animal already mid-carriageway is refused, because clearing it needs a human or a far-side emitter — never more volume.

Vulnerability vetoes

  • No escape corridor — pressure without an exit produces panic, not withdrawal. Requires 8 m of clear ground.
  • Juvenile present — real-world height from box + range. A calf means maternal defence and a more vulnerable animal.
  • Immobile after emission — moved <1.5 m? Possibly tethered, trapped or unwell. Pressing again is distress with no possible benefit.
  • Possibly downed — wide box aspect flags recumbency. Biased toward false positives on purpose.
  • Working animals — a bolting harnessed bullock endangers its handler, who is standing in the cone. Horses vetoed unconditionally.

Honest limit: juvenile classification degrades with range. In testing a calf was only classified correctly inside ~20 m, so this protection is weakest exactly where first contact happens.

Q6

“How do you know the sound reaches the animal?”

Because it is a link budget, not an assumption. Heffner & Heffner define the cattle hearing range at a 60 dB SPL criterion — so at the top of the band the signal must arrive at ≥60 dB, plus a margin above roadside noise. Working backwards through spreading and atmospheric absorption gives the level the emitter must produce, and whether that level is even legal.

Propagation validated against published values

Our model reproduces the source report’s attenuation table exactly, to 0.1 dB, at every distance.

Q7
“Isn’t this cruel to the animal?”

  
The only published cattle-ultrasound prototype reported 142 dB SPL at 1 m. That is roughly 27 dB above the international occupational ceiling for this band — over 500× the acoustic intensity. We treat that figure as a fault condition, not a specification.

    Q8
    “Will bystanders hear it?”
    
  
    A 25 kHz burst switched on and off abruptly is not a 25 kHz signal. The rectangular gate splatters broadband energy across the audible range, and the device clicks at every activation even though its carrier is ultrasonic. OSHA documents exactly this failure mode. So every burst is raised-cosine ramped, and the device FFTs its own buffer and refuses to play anything whose sub-20 kHz energy is too high.
    
  
    
    
    
    
      
    Carrier selection is constrained, not optimised for reach
    
      
    The optimiser maximises safety margin subject to four hard constraints, and returning “no feasible carrier” is a first-class outcome that triggers escalation rather than a louder emitter.

    • Must stay under the occupational ceiling

    • Modelled bystander audibility ≤ 2%

    • Must stay inside the documented 22–30 kHz band

    Selected carrier at the demo site: 22.0 kHz, chosen for margin, not for reach.
    
    
    
  
    

    


    
  
    Q9
    “Does it actually work?”
    
  
    We don’t know, and neither does anyone else. No published dose-response curve exists for cattle at 22–30 kHz. So the model returns credible intervals over an explicit prior — never a point estimate — and labels its own output uninformative when the prior dominates. This is the section other teams delete. It is the reason to trust the rest.

    Q10
    “What is it worth in traffic terms?”
    
  
    A blockage does not cost you the time it lasted — it costs you the queue it leaves behind, which takes longer to clear than the blockage itself. That is why clearing an animal sooner is worth far more than it first appears, and why we model it with standard deterministic queueing rather than an invented metric.

    Q12
    “What did you not build?”
    
  
    Stating this plainly is cheaper than being caught by it.
    
  
      
    
    • No hardware exists. No transducer, no amplifier, no field measurement. Every emission in this repository is marked dry_run: true, and a test asserts that no record anywhere claims otherwise.
      • 
    
    • No cattle were exposed to anything. The behavioural model is a prior, not a measurement, and says so in its own output.
      • 
    
    • The camera is uncalibrated. Distances come from inverse-perspective mapping with plausible defaults, carrying roughly ±20% error. Real deployment needs a measured homography.
      • 
    
    • The spectral check is a linear model. Real amplifiers and transducers add nonlinearity this does not capture. Only a calibrated ultrasonic microphone settles it.
      • 
    
    • Cattle welfare thresholds for 22–35 kHz do not exist in the literature. We borrow a human occupational ceiling because there is nothing better, and we label that substitution everywhere it appears.
